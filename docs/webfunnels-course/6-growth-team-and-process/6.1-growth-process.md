# Growth Team and Process

*Distilled from: «Курс по веб воронкам. 6 - Growth процесс» + lecture recording GMT20240707 (course module 6)*

## Summary

Revenue growth in web funnels comes from an experiment pipeline running at a steady cadence, not from individual clever ideas. Structure the team into goal-oriented squads (not functional silos), plan top-down from the financial model (year → quarter → month → 1–2 week growth sprint), and keep a conveyor of experiments moving through six stages: discovery, design, development, launch, analysis, rollout. The single most important operational KPI early on is the **number of experiments shipped per month** — test volume correlates directly with revenue growth; only once the conveyor runs reliably do you shift focus to win rate. A/B tests are judged on ARPU as the primary metric at 95% significance / 80% power, with guardrails against premature rollout decisions.

## Team structure

### Areas of responsibility

A growth team, under a growth leader, covers five zones:

| Zone | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| UA (user acquisition) | Paid traffic buying and creatives |
| Onboarding | Designing and testing quiz/onboarding flows |
| Payments / monetization | Paywalls, pricing, billing infrastructure |
| Support | Customer support; can own metrics such as refund rate |
| Ops | Coordination between teams — flow is not just leader-to-team but team-to-team |

### Key functional roles

Roles that directly move metrics (as opposed to infrastructure/billing work):

- **UA** — media buying.
- **Product marketing (PM)** — designs and tests onboarding and monetization experiments.
- **Analytics** — experiment design (audience, duration, metrics), result computation, verdicts.
- **Design** — the experiment's screens and flows.
- **Delivery (development/funnel building) + QA** — everything needed to actually ship the experiment.

**Roles are not headcount.** In early stages one person covers several roles (e.g., a founder who buys traffic, builds the onboarding, and analyzes results). Split them out as you scale.

### Squads over functions

Organize people around goals and tasks, not around function. Each squad contains a full loop: UA + PM + designer + analyst + delivery (developers or funnel-builder operators; payments can sit here too). Resources can be shared between squads, but every squad must own a common goal — e.g., one squad per funnel, per geo (US vs. non-US), or per direction.

## Planning

### Plan from the financial model

- The financial model produces company-wide and per-team goals, KPIs, and a decomposition into work directions.
- **The growth team's headline KPI is revenue growth**, decomposed into:
  - spend growth (with ROAS held),
  - conversion rate growth,
  - LTV growth,
  - churn reduction (cancellations, refunds).
- Build the yearly model with predictions first, then derive all planning from it; at each review compare plan vs. fact and either fix execution or fix the predictions.

### Planning horizons

| Horizon | Artifacts | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Year | High-level goals | Major company/team goals and directions |
| Quarter | OKRs + roadmap | Decomposed goals and initiatives per direction |
| Month | Month OKRs + roadmap | Concrete experiments (or finely decomposed directions) |
| Growth sprint (1–2 weeks) | Sprint plan | Plans and results for specific experiments |

### Growth roadmap vs. product roadmap

A growth roadmap lists **directions**, not features. On a year/quarter horizon you cannot know which specific experiments you will run, and they vary wildly in size — so plan directions such as:

- Funnel segmentation
- New funnel themes/topics
- Launching a new channel (e.g., Google)
- LTV growth through upsells

A product roadmap ("Feature 1, Feature 2, …") only makes sense at the month/sprint level, where the roadmap holds concrete experiments with ID, hypothesis, segment, priority score, target funnel, owner, and weekly status. Keep it in Notion, Google Sheets, or Jira/Confluence (see the templates doc, 6.2).

## Rhythms and rituals

Keeping the rhythm is more important than the specific format. Each planning horizon has its own loop:

**Quarterly rhythm:** OKR planning → backlog grooming → roadmap with directions → results (plan vs. fact against OKRs and predictions) + retro.

**Monthly rhythm:** month OKRs (derived from quarterly goals) → backlog grooming → roadmap with concrete experiments → results (plan vs. fact) + retro.

**Sprint rhythm (1–2 weeks):** experiment plan for the sprint → daily syncs (each squad moves its experiments through delivery, launch, analysis, rollout) → sprint results (what we planned vs. launched, what slipped and why) → retro that adapts the sprint process itself.

The sprint plan is split by experiment stage:

1. Discovery
2. Design
3. Development
4. Launch
5. Analysis
6. Rollout

The sprint team's core job is to keep pushing every experiment toward launch, analysis, and rollout — maximize launches and their quality.

## The growth sprint loop

The cycle is: **hypothesis generation → prioritization → test launch → results and takeaways → (back to generation)**.

Do not just count the numbers at the end of an experiment. Every experiment should answer "what did we learn?" — new insights are the primary source of the next hypothesis. Never launch a test without stating up front what you want to understand from it.

### Hypothesis generation — sources

1. **Competitors and adjacent funnel categories** — continuously monitor live funnels and steal applicable mechanics. Statistically one of the highest win-rate sources.
2. **Analytics** — run analytics research on the same sprint rhythm: long-term cohort analysis of past experiments, user behavior inside the funnel and the product (e.g., use in-product behavior to add storytelling or quiz questions to onboarding). Behavioral-analytics-sourced experiments have markedly higher success rates.
3. **User research.**
4. **External advising** — valuable less for concrete hypotheses than for an outside view; joint brainstorms are usually productive.

Public data (goodui.org) shows win rates differ by idea source: competitor copying and (behavioral) analytics lead. **Caution on "iterations":** re-running variations of a hypothesis that already failed rarely rescues it — don't keep "pushing" a dead hypothesis. Iterating on an experiment that *won* the first time usually keeps paying off.

### Involve the whole team in hypotheses

- **Immerse everyone in context:** joint reviews of experiment results and the metrics the team moves; discuss what worked, what didn't, and why.
- **Train designers (and builders/developers) as marketers.** Do it gradually: first give a concrete task with the hypothesis and mechanic explained ("we want to highlight the discount because …"), run a few feedback cycles, and over time they internalize which mechanics work and stop needing detailed specs.
- **Involve everyone in generation and prioritization.** Early hypotheses will be weak; the team learns. Eventually designers, analysts, and developers can generate hypotheses better than a newly hired PM, because they carry the accumulated context.

### Prioritization

- Most popular frameworks: **ICE** and **RICE**. ICE is recommended as the simplest. The specific framework doesn't matter — what matters is scoring hypotheses somehow and sorting them.
- **ICE components:**
  - *Impact* — how big is the potential effect? Don't guess: **calculate expected ARPU/ROAS/revenue growth with an experiment calculator** per hypothesis, then map onto your 1–10 scale.
  - *Confidence* — how sure are we? Grounded in facts: a competitor uses it, research supports it, etc.
  - *Effort* — how hard to implement? Count **all** effort, not just engineering: e.g., a video-onboarding test may be trivial to build but expensive to produce (actors, shooting, editing); include experiment run/analysis complexity too.
- Multiply (or sum — be consistent) the scores and take the highest-scoring initiatives first.

## Preparing and launching an experiment

### 1. Knowledge base

Describe every hypothesis in a written document (see the hypothesis template, 6.2). The accumulated knowledge base:

- Prevents losing learnings — at high test volume you will forget what you ran six months ago.
- Feeds generation of the next hypotheses.
- Onboards new team members: hand a new PM/analyst/designer the list of experiment docs with hypotheses, research, and results, and they absorb the context of what has been tried and what works.
- Enforces discipline: writing a hypothesis down (with an impact calculation) forces deeper thinking and at least minimal research, which raises win rate. "I would do it this way" is not a hypothesis; "top fitness funnels almost all use this mechanic" is.

### 2. Experiment design

- **Cheap, contrasty checks first.** Use fake doors — e.g., instead of a month-long PayPal integration, add a PayPal button to checkout and count clicks.
- **Test the most aggressive variant.** If the boldest version of the idea doesn't move metrics, a softer version certainly won't. (Even if you'd never roll out the aggressive version as-is.)
- **Define evaluation before launch:** how results will be judged, what you want to learn, how conclusions will be drawn.
- **Detailed design:** if there's screen-transition logic, put an explicit screen-flow into Figma.
- **Intro meeting:** the PM presents the experiment to the whole team before build starts.

### 3. Pre-launch testing

- Run a QA checklist: full funnel walk-through, test (or real) purchases, upsells work, form data isn't lost.
- **Run the funnel in native in-app browsers** (Instagram, TikTok, etc.).
- **QA analytics as its own deliverable:** verify events actually fire. Discovering mid-experiment that an event doesn't send means stopping and relaunching — always expensive.

### 4. Launch

- **Multiple channels? Launch on Meta first.** A failed test drags down campaign performance metrics; Google campaigns are especially slow and painful to recover — validate on Meta before exposing Google traffic.
- **High traffic? Ramp gradually:** start at ~20% of traffic and, if no anomalies, increase day by day.

## Operational KPI and monitoring

- **Test count correlates directly with revenue growth.** Set a KPI on the number of experiments run per month — initially on *run*, not on *won*. Getting a team to launch tests routinely, on a conveyor, is itself hard.
- Once the conveyor is stable, **work on win rate**: better hypothesis formulation, better execution (retros often reveal that good hypotheses die on weak design or implementation).

### Workflow monitoring

- Track each experiment's status through the pipeline (discovery → design → development → running → analysis → rollout/rollback) on a board.
- Watch for stuck stages — bottlenecks reveal resource constraints (e.g., not enough developers, or dev-heavy experiments starving the pipeline). Ops (or whoever owns the process role) unblocks them and plans resource load from the same board.

### Rhythm monitoring

Monthly/quarterly, review launched experiments and win rate **by cut**: per person, per team, per hypothesis source (which sources yield wins), per placement (paywall, pricing, quiz, monetization). Use this to double down on high-yield sources and placements.

### Throughput benchmarks (given sufficient traffic)

- **5 concurrent experiments per PM** — one at each stage (discovery, design, development, running, analysis).
- Resulting completed experiments: **~1/week, 4–5/month, 10–15/quarter** per PM. This is a middling pace — it can be pushed higher.
- **With a no-code funnel builder** (PM does only discovery + design; an analyst handles analysis): **3–4 tests/week = 12–16/month**.

## A/B testing

### Test setup checklist

1. **Target:** geo, demographics, specific funnels/segments — write it down explicitly (it's less obvious than it seems).
2. **Sample size and duration calculation** (use a calculator, links below).
3. **Analytics and metrics:** design the tracking, add any missing events, fix the decision metrics up front.
4. **Monitoring dashboard** built before launch (e.g., an Amplitude dashboard template reused per experiment).
5. **Measurement parameters:** significance 95%, power 80% (standard).

### Metrics

- **Primary: ARPU** (revenue per user across the whole population) — the final verdict is made on this metric.
- **Secondary:** conversion rate, ARPPU, cancellation rate, refund rate.

Why the full set matters: CR alone can be flat while plan mix shifts to a more expensive plan and ARPU grows; or CR "wins significantly" while refunds spike and eat the entire revenue effect (or worse). Judge the complex, conclude on ARPU.

### Avoiding "no effect after rollout"

Premature conclusions are the usual cause of an effect that vanishes at rollout:

- **Monitor p-value dynamics, not a single crossing.** P-value oscillates day to day; it can dip below the significance threshold and climb back out. Roll out only when the p-value has stabilized flat in the significant zone; otherwise keep the test running.
- **Monitor the effect size after rollout on a longer horizon.** A +30% revenue uplift at rollout may settle at +15% after 30 days — or grow (e.g., a monetization test that also improved subscription retention). Especially for pricing/monetization experiments, track both effect and p-value on long distances.

### Increasing test accuracy — large traffic volumes

- Raise significance to 99–100% (requires more traffic per variation).
- Budget **$5k–10k+ spend per variation** and **300–500+ sales per hypothesis**.
- Re-check cohorts after 3–6 months to confirm the effect was real.
- Hold a full seasonality cycle: minimum 7–14 days (weekly seasonality is typical).
- Watch for traffic mix changes during the test (channel split, country split) — day-to-day metric jumps often trace to traffic composition.
- Watch sample homogeneity; set alerts for skews (e.g., one variation getting disproportionately older, higher-paying users).
- After rolling out pricing tests or other large changes, **monitor traffic performance** — the ad auction can shift; re-check periodically.

### Increasing test accuracy — small traffic volumes

- Test **contrasty hypotheses with 30%+ potential uplift**: big storytelling changes, radically different or much shorter funnels, splitting the pre-paywall pitch into three screens — not two-screen tweaks. Prioritize ideas already proven by large players.
- Minimum **100 sales per variation** for a preliminary read.
- If a test shows **less than ~15% uplift, drop it and start the next test** — the true effect is likely ~half of the observed and unmeasurable at your volume.
- Everything else from the large-volume list still applies: cohort re-checks at 3–6 months, full seasonality cycle (7–14 days), traffic mix, sample homogeneity, post-rollout traffic performance.
