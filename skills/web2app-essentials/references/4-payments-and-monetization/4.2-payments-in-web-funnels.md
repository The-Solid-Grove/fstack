# 4.2 Payments in Web Funnels

*Distilled from course module 4 slides and lecture transcript.*

## Essential summary

Three pillars of payments in quiz-to-paywall funnels: (1) choosing the right **payment provider model** (PSP with your own merchant account vs. Merchant of Record) and knowing how far to go toward a custom payment stack; (2) actively managing **refunds, disputes, and card-network monitoring programs** so your merchant account survives; (3) building a **custom checkout and payment flow** — default hosted checkouts convert 20–30% worse, and most high-converting mechanics (paid trials, one-click upsells, upgrades) require custom logic on top of the providers.

## 1. Payment providers

### How an online payment flows

Buyer → your funnel (merchant) → payment gateway / acquirer → card network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, …) → the buyer's issuing bank, with the confirmation traveling back the same chain.

- **Merchant** — the digital ID banks use to identify the seller and process the payment (plus MCC category codes).
- **Payment gateway** — the payment provider; may also act as the acquirer.

### PSP vs. Merchant of Record

| | Payment Service Provider (PSP) — dedicated merchant | Merchant of Record (MoR) — shared merchant |
|---|---|---|
| Who sells, legally | You sell directly to the buyer under your own merchant account | The MoR sells to the buyer from its shared merchant; many sellers sit behind one provider |
| Examples | Stripe, Braintree, Solidgate, PayPal | Paddle, FastSpring (Stripe sits in between — mostly PSP, but offers MoR-style services) |
| Pros | Custom checkouts (checkout quality moves conversion 20–30%); full control over invoicing and customer emails (e.g. in Stripe you can disable invoice emails entirely); full control over the payment process and how the charge appears to the user | Almost no dispute-rate worries (the MoR handles disputes and even refunds for you, with softer thresholds); easy setup and fast start; no need to calculate/pay sales taxes yourself (MoR pays out net of taxes and fees); built-in multi-currency and geo-aware payment orchestration |
| Cons | You own the dispute rate and risk your merchant account (a blocked merchant means opening a new legal entity); you handle taxes; harder setup and customization; you handle regional differences (currencies, local payment methods) yourself | Checkout barely customizable — costs roughly **20–30% of conversion**; emails can't be configured (e.g. PayPal/Paddle always email users about every rebill → more churn, since it's an easy reminder to cancel); required MoR legal text clutters the checkout |

Note: PayPal always emails the user about charges and this can't be disabled even in PSP-style integrations; how much rebill emails increase churn depends on how aggressive your rebilling is — measure it.

### Payment orchestration

Orchestrators route each payment by rules: by provider, by payment method, by geo or any custom segment (you can even feed in your own quiz data). Example: a German user gets PayPal as the first/default method because PayPal's share in Germany is extremely high — the user doesn't have to switch methods manually, so less friction and higher conversion.

### Custom payment solution — four stages

1. **Direct integration with 3rd-party providers** (Stripe, Adyen, etc.): the provider hosts subscriptions and processing; you just consume webhooks for success/failure.
2. **Your own subscription management + PSP integration**: subscription entities, billing cycles, trial and intro-offer logic live on your side; you tell the provider "charge this customer this amount now."
3. **Subscription management + tokenization + direct processor integration**: you tokenize card data yourself and use a third-party processor for 3-D Secure and network submission.
4. **Fully custom payment provider including processing**: you handle 3DS per country/network rules yourself — you effectively become a fintech.

Most companies should stop at stage 1–2. Move toward stage 4 only when your payment **acceptance rate** is genuinely poor and worth the investment — it's a different business.

### Payment-flow optimizations (unlocked by deeper customization)

1. **Retry logic** — most declines are `insufficient_funds`. Beyond standard fixed retries (Stripe-style), use custom retries with **decreasing amounts** (try half, then a quarter, …). Services like **Butter Payments** specialize in fine-tuned retry logic, or build your own.
2. **Custom payment routing** — route US users to US merchant accounts: lower fees, better acceptance, no cross-border friction or currency conversion.
3. **Failover billing** — if the card charge fails, bill another payment method the user has on file.
4. **PayPal via Billing Agreement** — the legacy-but-reliable integration: the user signs an agreement to be charged, so the PayPal checkout doesn't display "subscription" wording on the button/screen — a simpler, less scary checkout. Prefer it over the vault-style flow where possible.
5. **Subscription management features** — pause/freeze (offer a 1-month pause instead of cancellation as a retention save), grace periods after failed payments (keep access so users don't churn on a technicality), upgrades/downgrades, bundles (sell two products/apps as one subscription, incl. partner bundles).
6. **Fraud and dispute tolerance** — with your own processing you can dilute risky transaction volume with clean transactions (e.g. from physical goods or other products) so the blended dispute rate stays acceptable to the networks. (Course-taught tactic; note it lives in a gray area with card-network rules.)

## 2. Refunds, disputes, and monitoring programs

### Refunds

The user contacts you and you return the money — no third party involved. Tips:

- Refunds are negotiable: offer a **partial refund (e.g. 50% back, framed as a discount)** — users often accept because their real objection is price vs. value. You keep a paying (if discounted) subscriber instead of losing everything.

### Disputes (chargebacks)

The user contests the transaction through their bank — often because they couldn't find how to reach you for a refund. The bank investigates (hours to days), may request evidence from both sides, and if it rules the transaction invalid it registers a dispute with the card network, which notifies your provider/merchant.

Key fact: **even if you contest a registered dispute and win, it still counts in your dispute-rate statistics.** Prevention beats winning.

### How dispute rate is calculated (Visa vs. Mastercard)

- **Visa (VAMP, since April 2025)**: (fraud reports + disputes) this month ÷ settled card-not-present transactions **this month** (not cohorted — they count against whatever volume you did this month).
- **Mastercard**: disputes this month ÷ transactions **previous month**.

Seasonality trap: after an aggressive high-season push (e.g. New Year), February volume drops while disputes from December–January transactions keep arriving — your ratio spikes without your behavior worsening. Always leave headroom below the thresholds; don't run at the edge.

### Card-network monitoring programs

> **Post-course update (verified July 2026): Visa replaced VDMP and VFMP with a single VAMP program.**
> On April 1, 2025 Visa retired VDMP and VFMP (the programs the course
> taught) and replaced both with a single global **Visa Acquirer Monitoring
> Program (VAMP)**; enforcement began October 1, 2025
> ([Stripe: monitoring programs](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)).
> The section below describes the current regime.

**Visa Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP)** — see https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs

- One combined ratio replaces the separate dispute and fraud programs:
  **VAMP ratio = (fraud reports TC40 + non-fraud disputes TC15) ÷ settled
  card-not-present transactions (TC05)**, same month, count-based. The same
  transaction can count twice (once as fraud, once as a dispute).
- Merchant level has a single **Excessive** tier, and you are identified only
  if you exceed **both** the ratio **and** a minimum of **1,500 combined
  fraud + disputes in the month** — small merchants are effectively exempt
  from direct identification. Acquirer-portfolio thresholds (0.5% Above
  Standard / 0.7% Excessive) still apply, so your acquirer/PSP will police
  you well below that count anyway.
- Merchant Excessive ratio (US, EU, Canada, AP): **≥2.2%** from June 2025,
  tightened to **≥1.5% since April 1, 2026**.
- Penalties are per-item fees billed to the acquirer and passed through:
  industry sources report **~$8 per fraud/dispute item** for an Excessive
  merchant (~$4 at acquirer Above Standard), with a 3-month grace period for
  first-time identification.
- **The alerts calculus changed.** RDR/CDRN pre-dispute resolutions still
  keep **non-fraud disputes (TC15)** out of the ratio, but **fraud TC40s
  count even when resolved via RDR/CDRN** — alert services no longer shield
  the fraud side at all. The only fraud carve-out is transactions qualified
  under Compelling Evidence 3.0.
- VAMP also monitors enumeration (card testing): ≥20% enumeration ratio and
  ≥300,000 enumerated authorizations/month (remediation-focused, no fines).

**Mastercard programs** — unchanged; see https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs#mastercard-programs

| Tier | Dispute count | Dispute rate | Fines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excessive Chargeback Merchant | 100–299 | 1.5–2.99% | Fines from month two, increasing monthly |
| High Excessive Chargeback Merchant | 300+ | 3% | Fines from month two, increasing monthly |

Mastercard added a third program on top of ECP/EFM: the **Scam Merchant
Monitoring Program (SMMP)**, enforced from **July 24, 2026** — combined
refunds + chargebacks above 5% of transactions over a rolling 30 days
(minimum 500 transactions) plus scam signals can end in termination of
Mastercard acceptance and MATCH listing. (Reported from Mastercard's July
2025 Security Rules & Procedures merchant edition; check the primary source
before relying on exact numbers.)

Getting into these programs is painful to exit, and failure to exit ends with
your merchant being blocked (i.e., a new legal entity to keep processing).
Monitor fraud and dispute rates continuously and keep a buffer.

### Payment statement descriptor

The descriptor is what the user sees on their bank statement (company name + transaction label; banks even auto-attach logos/icons).

- **Unclear descriptor → user can't identify you → can't request a refund → files a dispute instead.** Dispute rate goes up.
- It's a balancing tool (PSP-only — an MoR controls its own descriptor): if disputes are your problem, make the descriptor maximally clear so users come to you for refunds; if refunds are the bigger cost and disputes are fine, a less obvious descriptor shifts the balance back. Adjust over time to your situation.

### Dispute prevention / alert services

Mechanism: **Verifi (owned by Visa)** and **Ethoca (owned by Mastercard)** receive early notifications from issuing banks that a dispute is being raised. Alert services (e.g. **Chargeblast**, **Chargeback Gurus**, and similar resellers — or your own direct Verifi/Ethoca integration) match the alert to your transaction and **refund it immediately**, so when the bank finishes its investigation the transaction is already refunded and **no dispute is ever registered with Visa/Mastercard** — it never enters your dispute-rate statistics.

- Pricing: roughly **$32–40 per alert/prevented dispute**.
- Tools have essentially identical coverage (same two data sources); they differ in integration quality, transaction-matching accuracy, and price.

## 3. Checkout and payment flow

### Payment-method mix (benchmark)

| Method | Share |
|---|---|
| Apple Pay | ~60% (when set as the well-designed default) |
| Cards | ~20% |
| PayPal | ~20% |
| Google Pay | ~1–2% |

Geo caveats:

- **Germany: PayPal share can reach ~90%** — set it as the default there.
- Historically Apple Pay didn't work in Facebook/Instagram in-app browsers and PayPal took >50% of payments there; Apple Pay now works in those webviews and has reclaimed share.
- **US: Stripe Link** is popular — not a separate method but Stripe's card-detail vault that autofills saved cards for effectively one-click card payments.

### Checkout design

**Default hosted checkouts (Stripe, Paddle) convert 20–30% worse** than custom checkouts. They aren't optimized for your product: full price displayed in large type, generic non-customizable buttons ("Subscribe" vs. "Continue" perform very differently), extra steps (Paddle asks for email, then country/zip on a second screen), and MoR legal text.

Custom checkout rules:

- **Minimize friction ruthlessly** — ideally one button, one step.
- **Preselect the most suitable payment method** (usually Apple Pay) and make it visually dominant — e.g. the Apple Pay button *is* the CTA.
- Keep other methods merely visible with little visual weight (PayPal, card icons quietly signal "we have these too").
- No unnecessary fields, buttons, or options.
- Add trust icons: secure payment, https, "we don't store your card details," etc.

Where to show the checkout (all worth testing):

- A separate funnel screen.
- A modal over the paywall.
- **Directly on the paywall** — has been performing very well recently across many projects.

### Payment-mechanic support matrix (with custom checkouts)

| Mechanic | Stripe | Paddle | PayPal | Custom payments layer (built in-house) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Introductory offer (discounted first billing cycle) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Free trial (e.g. 1 week free, then $29.99/month) | Custom logic needed | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Paid trial (e.g. 1-week trial $4.99, then $29.99/month) | No — build your own | No — build your own | Yes | Yes |
| One-click upsell (extra sale on top of the subscription) | No — build your own | No — build your own | No — build your own | Yes |
| Subscription upgrade (replace purchased plan with a pricier one) | No — build your own | No — build your own | No — build your own | In development |

Implementation notes per provider:

- **Intro offers**: Stripe — create a coupon and apply it to the first billing cycle only; Paddle — same idea, called "discounts"; PayPal — set an intro period with its own price when creating the subscription.
- **Free trial**: Stripe — with a custom checkout you can't just set a free period on the subscription; it needs extra API logic. Paddle — trial period is set on the price object. PayPal — intro period priced at $0.
- **Paid trial**: only PayPal supports it natively (intro period priced at $X); on Stripe and Paddle you must build the trial-to-subscription conversion yourself (e.g. 1 week at $4.99 converting into a 1- or 3-month subscription).
- **One-click upsell**: the whole point is *no checkout shown* — the user already bought and chose a payment method; charge that method with a single button press. No provider supports this out of the box.
- **PayPal cannot do one-time purchases at all.** For one-time upsells after a PayPal subscription purchase, the course authors integrated **Braintree** and ran one-click one-time upsells through it.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Chosen PSP vs. MoR deliberately: MoR for fast/low-risk start, PSP for conversion control at scale
- [ ] Custom checkout: one step, Apple Pay preselected and dominant, trust icons, minimal fields
- [ ] Geo-aware method routing (PayPal default in Germany; US traffic on US merchant accounts)
- [ ] Decline handling: retries (incl. decreasing amounts), failover to a second method, grace periods
- [ ] Pause offer and partial-refund (50%) script in the cancellation/refund flow
- [ ] Clear statement descriptor; dispute rate tracked weekly against Visa 0.65%/0.9% and Mastercard thresholds with headroom
- [ ] Dispute alert service (Verifi/Ethoca-based) wired to auto-refund, ~$32–40 per prevented dispute
- [ ] Custom logic for paid trials, one-click upsells, and upgrades; Braintree fallback for one-time upsells on PayPal purchases
