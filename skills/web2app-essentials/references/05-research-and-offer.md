# 5. Audience research and offer creation

## Lesson outcome

You'll prepare a funnel brief: one segment, one entry promise, one transformation, and an offer you can test.

## Don't start with screens

A quiz is an interface. The funnel starts earlier: with understanding why someone clicks, what they have already tried, and what they fear.

Fill in a research map before you design anything.

| Area | What to collect |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger evidence | the specific situations in which the user starts looking for a solution |
| Failed attempts and barriers | what they have already tried, and why they hesitate, delay, or don't pay |
| Observable progress | what exactly they want to get and to see |
| Emotional stakes | why the result gives control, calm, confidence, or another meaningful outcome |
| Proof available | which product data, demonstrations, and customer evidence the team can use honestly |

From the table, select:

- **primary problem** — the problem that creates click intent;
- **mechanism** — the explanation of why the old approach doesn't work and how the new one helps;
- **point B** — the observable change;
- **entry promise** — what the creative promises and the first screen confirms;
- **offer** — what the buyer gets, for how long, and on what terms.

## Where to look for evidence

### Your own data

- onboarding answers;
- cancellation reasons;
- support tickets;
- first-session behavior;
- differences between retained and churned cohorts;
- search queries;
- top creatives and the comments under them.

### Conversations

Run interviews with more than just satisfied users. These groups are especially useful:

- recent buyers;
- people who saw the paywall but didn't buy;
- people who canceled before the first renewal;
- retained users;
- people who chose a competitor.

Ask about events, not opinions:

- "What was happening on the day you decided to look for a solution?"
- "What had you tried before that?"
- "What made you hesitate before paying?"
- "What did you expect to see right after the purchase?"

### Market observation

Study active creatives and live funnels in your category and adjacent ones. Look beyond the design:

- what awareness level the first screen assumes;
- which questions create segmentation;
- where the funnel gives feedback;
- what result appears before the paywall;
- how the offer connects to the declared goal;
- what happens after checkout.

A long-running creative or funnel is a signal to investigate, not proof of profitability.

## Promise chain

Write down a single chain:

```text
Creative promise
→ first-screen confirmation
→ questions that diagnose the situation
→ evidence/mechanism
→ personalized result
→ offer
→ first product action
```

Every transition has to answer the user's question: "why does the next step make sense?"

If the creative talks about screen-time addiction and the first screen suddenly sells a generic productivity plan, the expectation breaks. If the quiz diagnoses one problem and the paywall offers a bundle of unrelated features, the offer looks generic.

## Personalization with consequences

Weak personalization drops a name into the headline.

Strong personalization uses the answer to change:

- the next question;
- the feedback;
- the recommended routine;
- the result summary;
- the plan duration;
- the paywall emphasis;
- the first useful action in the app.

In a real funnel, we used branching not to create a feeling of "AI analysis," but so that different answers would open different explanations and value moments. A branch like that is only justified when the content genuinely differs.

## An offer is not a price card

Describe the offer before you build the paywall:

```text
For [segment] who wants [outcome],
we provide [mechanism/deliverables]
over [time horizon],
starting with [first useful action].
The customer pays [today price],
then [renewal terms].
```

The offer has to explain:

- the result or progress the buyer gets;
- what paid access includes;
- why the duration fits the job;
- what happens immediately after payment;
- how much is charged today;
- when and how much will be charged next;
- how to cancel and request a refund.

## How to choose your first funnel angle

Score candidates on four factors:

1. **Demand:** the problem already creates search/click intent.
2. **Proof:** the product has evidence or a mechanism you can show honestly.
3. **Delivery:** the product delivers on the promise and creates first value quickly.
4. **Economics:** the offer has a chance of paying back paid traffic.

Pick one angle. Your first funnel should not cover the entire product catalog.

## Choose a funnel archetype

The archetype defines the work the web experience does before the offer.

| Archetype | What it does | When it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assessment | diagnoses the situation and shows the result | answers genuinely change the interpretation or recommendation |
| Plan builder | collects constraints and assembles a first plan | the value can be made concrete before the app is installed |
| Calculator | shows the cost of inaction, a potential outcome, or a range | inputs and assumptions can be explained honestly |
| Guided demo | demonstrates the product mechanism through a short scenario | the product is clearer in action than as a feature list |
| Direct response | quickly explains problem, proof, and offer | intent is already high and a long quiz only adds friction |

Don't pick a quiz by default. If the answers don't change the result, direct response or a guided demo is usually more honest and shorter.

## What not to copy from other funnels

- invented testimonials;
- unsupported projections;
- fake progress loaders that claim analysis that isn't happening;
- artificial discounts with no real reference price;
- a timer that restarts forever;
- a guarantee that support won't honor;
- urgency that isn't tied to a real offer.

These mechanics can lift short-term conversion and destroy retention, reputation, and your payment account at the same time.

## Exercise

Create a one-page brief:

1. segment and trigger event;
2. problem and failed alternative;
3. functional and emotional outcome;
4. creative promise;
5. 5–8 questions and why each answer is needed;
6. result preview;
7. offer and billing terms;
8. first useful action;
9. evidence you can use;
10. claims you cannot use.
